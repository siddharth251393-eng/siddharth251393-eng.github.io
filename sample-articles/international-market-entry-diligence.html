Market entry diligence should include impacts and dependencies

Editorial demonstration — not published news.

This sample is provided to show IndustryWire’s intended explanatory style. It is based on the public references listed below but has not completed independent editorial verification. It does not describe a current company event, proprietary research result or market forecast.

Sample prepared 16 November 2026 · No author byline assigned

A market-entry plan usually tests customer demand, distribution, pricing, competition and regulation. A broader diligence process also asks how the company’s operations and business relationships may affect people and the environment, and what responsibilities follow from those impacts.

The OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct describes six connected steps: embed responsible business conduct in policies and management systems; identify and assess impacts; cease, prevent or mitigate them; track implementation and results; communicate how impacts are addressed; and enable remediation when appropriate. OECD guidance says the approach should be risk-based and proportionate to an enterprise’s circumstances.

Use the framework early

Before selecting distributors, contractors or sourcing partners, map the relationships that matter to the proposed activity. Identify where information is missing, which impacts could be severe, and what evidence is needed before a commitment is made. Do not treat a signed supplier code as proof that risks have been identified or addressed.

Separate legal gates from business assumptions

List licences, ownership rules, customs requirements, product approvals, data restrictions and employment obligations by jurisdiction. Mark the source and date for each requirement and assign a local review owner. Commercial assumptions such as addressable demand or partner capacity should be tracked separately from legal requirements.

Close the loop after launch

Assign owners and timelines for mitigations, grievance channels, monitoring and escalation. Revisit the assessment when the operating model, partners or country conditions change. This turns diligence into a management process rather than a one-time entry checklist.
